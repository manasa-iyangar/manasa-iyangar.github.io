01 · Lesson design

Interactive IGCSE science learning

Grade 6 · Biology · 45 minutes

Animal classification: evidence detectives

Purpose: Compare animals using observable characteristics and justify a classification with evidence.

Learning outcomes

  • Distinguish mammals from other animal groups using more than one feature.
  • Use teeth, body covering, footprints, feeding and movement as evidence.
  • Explain why a single characteristic may not be enough to classify an organism.

Learning sequence

  1. Hook (5 min): Show three mystery footprints and invite learners to infer the animal, explaining uncertainty.
  2. Evidence stations (12 min): Teams rotate through image cards showing body covering, teeth, habitat and feeding clues.
  3. Classification challenge (15 min): Groups classify unfamiliar animals and record the evidence behind each choice.
  4. Challenge (8 min): Discuss a feature that can mislead, such as an egg-laying mammal.
  5. Exit ticket (5 min): State a claim, give two pieces of evidence and explain one limitation.
Assessment evidence: Accuracy of classification, quality of evidence and ability to explain uncertainty.

Grade 8 · Biology · 45 minutes

Nutrition across organisms

Purpose: Compare how Amoeba, fungi and humans obtain and process nutrients.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe holozoic and saprotrophic nutrition with examples.
  • Sequence the key steps in nutrition in Amoeba and humans.
  • Connect structure to function using scientific vocabulary.

Learning sequence

  1. Do now (5 min): Sort examples into “makes its own food” and “obtains food from other sources”.
  2. Model and compare (10 min): Trace ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation and egestion where applicable.
  3. Team task (15 min): Complete a comparison grid for Amoeba, fungi and humans.
  4. Misconception check (10 min): Explain why fungi digest food externally before absorbing nutrients.
  5. Exit ticket (5 min): Compare two organisms using one similarity and two differences.
Assessment evidence: Completed comparison grid and a concise, accurate explanation.

Chemistry · Grade 10 · ICSE-style

Mole concept: worked example and practice

Sample problem: Calculate the amount, in moles, present in 11 g of carbon dioxide. (C = 12, O = 16)

Worked solution

  1. Molar mass of CO₂ = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44 g mol⁻¹.
  2. Amount in moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
  3. n = 11 ÷ 44 = 0.25 mol.

Extension questions

  1. Calculate the number of molecules in 0.25 mol of CO₂.
  2. Find the mass of 0.5 mol of CaCO₃. (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)
  3. A learner says that 1 mol of every substance has the same mass. Explain the error.
Design principle: Move from a familiar calculation to transfer, explanation and misconception diagnosis.

Chemistry · Equation practice

Balancing equations: from routine to reasoning

Student task: Balance each equation using the smallest whole-number coefficients, then identify the reaction pattern.

  1. __ Mg + __ O₂ → __ MgO
  2. __ Al + __ HCl → __ AlCl₃ + __ H₂
  3. __ Fe₂O₃ + __ CO → __ Fe + __ CO₂
  4. __ C₃H₈ + __ O₂ → __ CO₂ + __ H₂O
  5. __ KClO₃ → __ KCl + __ O₂

Reflection prompt

Explain why balancing an equation changes coefficients but not the chemical formulae of the substances.

Teacher look-fors: Conservation of atoms, correct coefficient ratios and an explanation that distinguishes coefficients from subscripts.

02 · Assessment design

Questions that reveal thinking

Environmental Science · Grade 9

Climate, albedo and permafrost: a HOTS prompt

Scenario: A region experiences a decline in reflective snow cover while temperatures rise. Nearby permafrost begins to thaw.

Questions

  1. Explain how reduced snow cover can affect the energy absorbed by the surface.
  2. Construct a cause-and-effect chain linking warming, permafrost thaw and greenhouse gases.
  3. Identify two pieces of data that would help test whether the feedback is occurring.
  4. Explain one limitation of concluding that a single warm year proves long-term climate change.
Assessment focus: Causal reasoning, evidence selection, feedback loops and evaluation of claims.

Assessment framework

Formative feedback and success criteria

Use a short, repeatable rubric to make feedback specific and actionable.

  • Concept: Is the scientific idea accurate?
  • Evidence: Does the learner use observations, data or examples?
  • Reasoning: Is the connection between evidence and conclusion explained?
  • Communication: Are terms, units, diagrams and equations used appropriately?
Feedback stem: “Your explanation correctly identifies ___. To strengthen it, connect ___ to ___ using evidence from ___.”

03 · Science communication

Resources for visual and practical learning

Biology · Ecology

Food-chain investigation

Task: Learners build food chains from organism cards, use arrows to show the direction of energy transfer, and combine chains into a food web.

Challenge prompts

  • Predict two effects if a primary consumer population declines.
  • Explain why arrows point from food to consumer.
  • Identify an organism that could occupy more than one trophic level and justify the answer.
Deliverable: Annotated food web plus a written prediction supported by reasoning.
Biology · Adaptation

Structure, habitat and survival

Learners compare organisms from marine, desert, polar and forest habitats using body shape, covering, movement, feeding structures and behaviour.

Evidence table headings

  • Observed feature
  • Habitat condition
  • Possible survival advantage
  • Evidence and alternative explanation
Extension: Explain why an adaptation that is useful in one environment may be less useful in another.
04 · Curriculum planning

From curriculum map to learning sequence

Planning framework

Unit mapping and time allocation

A practical planning sequence for organising a unit before teaching begins.

  1. List required topics and curriculum outcomes.
  2. Identify prerequisite knowledge and essential concepts.
  3. Group related concepts into teachable sequences.
  4. Estimate time for explanation, investigation, practice and assessment.
  5. Build in retrieval, feedback and reteaching opportunities.
  6. Review pacing against learner evidence and adjust.
Planning check: Does each lesson produce evidence that learners are moving towards the unit outcomes?
Classroom environment

Learning corners and classroom agreements

Plan the classroom as a supportive learning environment with purposeful spaces and shared expectations.

  • Reading corner: independent reading and science-text exploration.
  • Calm corner: a quiet reset space with clear, respectful routines.
  • Nature corner: observation objects, plant care and seasonal questions.
  • Creative corner: diagrams, models, prototypes and student explanations.
Success indicator: Learners can explain how to use each space and take shared responsibility for it.
